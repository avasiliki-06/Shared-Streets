01 / THE PROBLEM

Who gets
the street?

A sustainable city is not only about where people are going. It is about whether they can get there safely, comfortably, and reliably.

CLIENT: UNITED NATIONS SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT GOAL 11 — SUSTAINABLE CITIES AND COMMUNITIES. Shared Streets is the proposed Toronto campaign, translating the client’s commitment to inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable cities into a focused call for safer, more accessible transportation infrastructure. Walking, cycling, and public transit need safe, accessible, and reliable infrastructure to work for everyone.

Research from the Toronto Census Metropolitan Area shows that the reported share of commuters using sustainable transportation declined from 19.3% in 2016 to 13.9% in 2021. Public transit declined from 24.3% to 15.6%, while active transportation declined from 6.7% to 5.7%. These figures establish the central issue explored by the campaign.

THE PROPOSAL: take the message out of the report and put it directly into the city.

Shared Streets is a public-space campaign proposed for UN SDG 11. It uses oversized, confrontational messages and site-specific installations to question who urban streets are designed for. Instead of asking commuters to seek out the campaign online, the proposal brings the message directly into their daily routes.

THE MEDIA STRATEGY / WHY THESE LOCATIONS

The location
is the message.

This campaign does not ask people to stop what they are doing and find it. It meets them at two moments already built into their commute: the pause before crossing and the wait for transit. Those choices make the campaign relevant to the space, the audience, and the issue.

01 / TYPOGRAPHY

Make the message hit first.

I used Filicudi as the typeface for the installation headlines. I chose it for its bold, distinctive letterforms and strong visual personality, which give the campaign a recognizable voice rather than making it feel like a standard transit notice. At the large scale, Filicudi helps the words hold their own against busy photography, station architecture, vehicle movement, and other visual distractions. Its weight and character make the message feel assertive and immediate, while keeping the campaign visually memorable from a distance.

Questions and short statements such as “WHO IS THE STREET FOR?” and “MAKE ROOM TO MOVE” are direct by design. They are meant to stop people for a moment and make them reconsider how much space is given to cars compared with people walking, cycling, and taking transit.

02 / COLOUR

High contrast. High visibility.

Orange, black, and off-white create a strong contrast that helps the campaign stand out in visually busy public spaces. The orange also connects the work to the selected SDG 11 identity, while the darker tones echo asphalt, transit infrastructure, and the urban environment.

04 / REFLECTION

Where do

A safer destination starts with the choices we make about the journey.

Project reflection

Working on Shared Streets helped me understand how transportation data can be connected to the everyday experience of moving through a city. The research showed that the reported share of sustainable transportation in the Toronto CMA declined between 2016 and 2021. It also showed that 50.5% of commuters travelled for 30 minutes or longer in 2021. These findings made the issue feel less distant because they relate directly to the time people spend travelling and the streets they use every day. I wanted the campaign to reach people while they were already commuting, rather than asking them to seek out information somewhere else.

The installation locations became an important part of the communication strategy. A subway station reaches people while they wait for transit, while an intersection places the message in the same shared space being questioned by the campaign. The building visualization in the subway installation was developed to resemble a bar graph, allowing the architecture of the city to become part of the data presentation. This helped connect the information to its physical setting instead of presenting it as a chart that could exist anywhere.

Typography was also an important part of the campaign’s visual identity. I chose Filicudi for the installation designs because its bold, distinctive letterforms give the message a strong presence within busy urban environments. The type needed to be noticed quickly by people moving through a subway station or waiting at an intersection. Its visual character helps make “MAKE ROOM TO MOVE” feel direct and confident, while creating a clear contrast against the photographic backgrounds. Rather than treating typography as something added after the message was written, I used it as part of the campaign’s art direction: it attracts attention, establishes the tone, and helps the message remain memorable.

Ruben Pater’s discussion of information graphics in The Politics of Design encouraged me to consider that visual communication is shaped by cultural and political context, rather than being neutral. This was relevant to a campaign about who streets are designed to serve. The way transportation information is selected and presented can influence which parts of the issue people notice, so I aimed to make the campaign’s position clear while keeping the supporting data visible.

Hullman and Diakopoulos explain that the design of a visualization can frame how an audience interprets information. Their discussion made me think more carefully about the relationship between the data, the visual form, the written message, and the order in which viewers encounter them. In this campaign, the direct question attracts attention first, while the statistics and visualizations provide context. This sequence is persuasive, but it also means I have a responsibility to represent the research accurately and identify where the information comes from.

RJ Andrews’s chapter “Embodied Encoding: The Power of Metaphor” considers how visual metaphors can help make information understandable. This connected to my decision to shape the subway building graphic so that it also reads as a bar graph. The familiar form of a city skyline gives the data a physical reference and makes the visualization feel connected to the place where it appears. It also reinforced the importance of choosing a visual metaphor that supports the information rather than distracting from it.

Overall, this project taught me that a campaign about public space needs to consider both what it communicates and where people encounter it. The strongest decisions came from connecting the research, the visual language, and the installation environments into one clear message. If I continued developing the campaign, I would test the installations at viewing distance and with different audiences to see whether the data and message are understood quickly, and I would further evaluate how effectively the campaign encourages people to think about safer and more accessible transportation.
